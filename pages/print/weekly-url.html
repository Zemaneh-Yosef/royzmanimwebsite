The Month of Tishri

בֶּחֳדָשִׁים הַשְּׁבִיעִי חָבִיב, "בַחֹדֶשׁ הַשְּׁבִיעִי בְּאֶחָד לַחֹדֶשׁ"
Of the months, the seventh is beloved; "In the seventh month, on the first day"
Yalkut Shimoni, Parashat Yitro 276

Tishri starts several cycles – Shemitta, vows, planting ('Orla) and vegetation (tithes, terumot) – and per R' Eliezer, the world (determined by mankind) was created in this month, or at least creation as a concept occurred for God then (Rabbenu Tam, Rosh Hashana 27a; cf. Ḥagiga 12a, Rashi on Bereshit 1:1, Bereshit Rabba 12:4). Fittingly, its name derives from the Akkadian tašrītu ("beginning"), from šurrū (to begin or open).

Its holidays center on judgment, repentance and God's kingship: Rosh Hashana (shofar) and Yom Kippur (a full fast), joined by the 'Aseret Yemei Teshuva, focus on our judgment; Sukkot (sukka and four species) turns to the judgment of rain (cf. the month's old name, איתנים – I Melakhim 8:2), culminating in Shemini 'Atzeret, when (in Israel) we also complete the Torah cycle.

Its history opens with the 'Akeida (2085/1677 BCE by tradition) – on the 1st (Peshat; serves as an explanation to various practices of Rosh Hashana), or the 10th (Zohar). The Midrashim link this event with Sara's passing, culmunating with the purchase of מערת המכפלה (Rashi, Bereshit 23:2; Shadal does not necessarily connect the two). Later came the First Temple's dedication, celebrated through Sukkot (I Melakhim 8:65; 2936/826 BCE by tradition), and, after its destruction, the murder of Gedalya ben Aḥikam, ending Jewish self-rule – mourned with Tzom Gedalya on the 3rd (II Melakhim 25:25; 3339/423 BCE by tradition).

The Month of Marḥeshvan

In the Tanakh (I Melakhim 6:38), this month is referred to as Yeraḥ Bul, a name rooted in seasonal shifts: the withering of grass (baleh), the mixing of fodder (bolelin), and the harvesting of produce (yevul). The name is also associated with the onset of rain – forming clods of earth (bolot) and recalling the Great Flood (mabul), which began on the 17th of this month (Bereshit 7:11; 1656/2106 BCE by tradition). This mirrors the practice in Israel of requesting rain in ברך עלינו from the 7th of the month (elsewhere, from early December), with fasts once held if none fell by the 17th.

The name Marḥeshvan (often shortened to Ḥeshvan) instead denotes the month's numerical order, deriving from the Akkadian warḫu samnu ("eighth month"). Linguistically, warḫu corresponds to yeraḥ (month) and samnu to shemona (eight). While the prefix Mar is often interpreted as "bitter" due to the month's lack of festivals, it also means "drop" (as in Yesha'yahu 40:15), signifying the welcome arrival of the autumn rains. Conversely, the Aramaic definition behind the prefix "Mar" is "master", alluding to the First Temple, which King Shelomo completed in this month (2935/827 BCE by tradition; c. 959 BCE by historians) – despite the bitter delay of its dedication, the month will eventually be repaid with future celebration.

The Month of Kislev

The strength of Kislev appears as early as Ḥaggai, who encouraged the nation to rebuild the Temple, promising prosperity and victory over its era's enemies (Ḥaggai 2:10; 24th of Kislev, 3408/354 BCE by tradition; 520 BCE by historians). The later scribe 'Ezra showed further strength, gathering the nation within three days to strengthen Torah observance and dissolve interfaith marriages ('Ezra 10:9; 20th of Kislev, 3414/348 BCE by tradition; 458 BCE by historians).

The month's most famous display of strength was the war against the Greek empire, celebrated as Ḥanukka – by lighting candles for eight nights, reciting the full Hallel, and adding 'Al Ha'Nissim. Although the story spanned several years, its milestones fell in this month:

  • On the 15th, the "abomination of desolation" was set on the altar and idols were set in the streets of Judea; on the 25th, idolatrous sacrifices were offered on the altar (3619/143 BCE by tradition; 167 BCE by historians).
  • Three years later, on the 25th, the Kohanim resumed their service, and the only sealed oil jug found lit the Menorah for eight days (3622/140 BCE by tradition; 164 BCE by historians).

The name Kislev itself may echo this theme of strength. Some derive it from the Akkadian root kislu (kaslu), meaning "inner strength."

The Month of Tevet

Following Ḥanukka, Tevet serves as the representative of the winter season, whether as its climax or beginning (see Bava Metzia 106b). The winter solstice found in this month (as titled in the Hebrew – Tekufat Tevet) brings the northern hemisphere's shortest days, as the sun takes its lowest path across the sky. This detail could be reflected in the original name of the month, which is derived from Akkadian ṭebētu ("sunk").

Spiritually, this solar gloom reflects a deeper Kabbalistic theme. According to the Zohar (Yitro 13:241) and Ma'aseh Roke'aḥ (commentary to Mishna Ta'anit 4:6), Tevet is one of three months governed by stern judgment (Dina Kasya). In the division of the months between Ya'akov and 'Esav, Tevet fell into the portion of 'Esav, temporarily giving power to the Sitra Aḥara (the side of impurity).

Within the month itself, this spiritual vulnerability manifests on the 8th–10th days, known for the tragedies that befell – namely the translation of the Torah into Greek (c. 3rd century BCE by historians), the passing of 'Ezra (and with it, the leadership he represented), and the siege of Jerusalem (II Melakhim 25:1; 3336/425 BCE by tradition; 588 BCE by historians), marked by the fast of צום העשירי from dawn until nightfall. It was also in this month, decades after the siege, that King Aḥashverosh took Esther as his wife (Megillat Esther 2:16; 3399/362 BCE by tradition; 478 BCE by historians), setting in motion the Purim story and the redemption that followed.

The Month of Shevat

שנשמע בשורות טובות

That we may hear good tidings

The agricultural influence of Shevat is found as early as its name, with its Akkadian source (šabāṭu) referring to the harsh rains that come with the solar season of this month. We also know (from R' Hai Gaon, Teshuvot Ha'Geonim [Harkavy], §243) that the solar season coincides with the Arabic al-jamra al-thāniya (the second ember/coal) period, where the trees become moist and begin to absorb water, thus reviving them. Hence, it is fitting for the New Year of the Trees to fall in this season, celebrated on the 15th day known as ט"ו בשבט. (The exact date follows the ruling of בית הלל; see Mishna Rosh Hashana 1:1 for the full discussion, including the opinion of בית שמאי.)

On the 1st of this month, Moshe began his final speech, addressing the nation before Yehoshua replaced his leadership (Devarim 1:3; 2488/1273 BCE by tradition). These teachings would ultimately be written in the form of Sefer Devarim, often called Mishneh Torah.

The Month of Adar I

The months of the year are determined by the lunar cycle, and the lunar year falls about 11 days short of the solar year. This desync causes a problem for Jewish holidays, which have seasonal placements despite their dates being set by the lunar month. Thus, in leap years, we add the month of Adar I to the calendar. With Hillel's calendar cycle originating in 358–9 CE, the institution of this month was no longer dependent on agricultural concerns and astronomical calculations (which required a Sanhedrin), instead following a fixed pattern of seven leap years and twelve "ordinary" years, collectively becoming a nineteen-year cycle.

We've kept the month of Adar II for the respective festival of Purim (to keep the redemption of Purim close to the redemption from Egypt) and the recital of the Arba Parashiot (which are dependent on Purim). Nevertheless, the 14th and 15th of Adar I are called [Shushan-]Purim Katan, observed by refraining from Taḥanun, eulogies, or fasts. The name Adar itself derives from the Akkadian addaru; in Hebrew, the root אדר denotes might and glory (as in אדיר).

The ramifications of this month affect Hiloulot, vows, and birthdays (affecting Jewish adulthood – the bar mitzvah status). (See the discussion in Talmud Yerushalmi, Megilla 7a–7b.)

The Month of Adar (II)

מִשֶּׁנִּכְנַס אֲדָר מַרְבִּין בְּשִׂמְחָה
When [the month of] Adar begins, one increases rejoicing.
Talmud Bavli, Ta'anit 29a

Adar centers on Purim, the overturning of Haman's decree to annihilate the Jews of Persia. Though Haman had been executed, his decree stood, so the Jews were authorized to defend themselves: they fought on the 13th throughout the empire and rested on the 14th; in Shushan, they fought through the 14th and rested on the 15th (3405/356 BCE by tradition; 473 BCE by historians).

Purim, recorded in Megillat Esther, falls on the 15th in cities walled since the days of Yehoshua (as well as Shushan), and the 14th elsewhere. Observance includes reading Megillat Esther, giving to the poor, sending food gifts, and a festive meal (with wine – drink a bit more than usual, then sleep; see B"Y 695:2). Ta'anit Esther, held beforehand, recalls the fasting and prayer that preceded the fighting.

Adar also marks victories from both Temple eras: Nevukhadnetzar, destroyer of the First Temple, died on the 25th (Rashi, II Melakhim 25:27; 3364/397 BCE by tradition; 562 BCE by historians); and the Second Temple-era Battle of Adasa is commemorated as Yom Nikanor on the 13th (I Maccabees 7:26–50; 161 BCE by historians). The 7th marks both the birth and the passing of Moshe Rabbenu (Megilla 13b; 2368/1393 and 2488/1273 BCE by tradition).

The Month of Nisan

הַחֹ֧דֶשׁ הַזֶּ֛ה לָכֶ֖ם רֹ֣אשׁ חֳדָשִׁ֑ים רִאשׁ֥וֹן הוּא֙ לָכֶ֔ם לְחָדְשֵׁ֖י הַשָּׁנָֽה:
This month shall be to you the head of the months; to you it shall be the first of the months of the year.
Shemot 12:2

Nisan is a month of new beginnings. Its name derives from the Akkadian Nisānu, and it marks several fresh starts:

  • It opens spring (hence its biblical name, חודש האביב), ending winter and allowing new produce to grow.
  • Kings' reigns are counted from this month when dating official documents. (Mishna Rosh Hashana 1:1)

Most significant is the Jewish nation's own transition from slavery in Egypt to freedom, commemorated as Pesaḥ (2448/1313 BCE by tradition). The nation sanctified the new moon of Wednesday night as the start of Nisan (the first month ever sanctified). The following Shabbat (the 10th), the nation was commanded to tie a lamb to their bedpost, alarming the Egyptians into internal quarrel; this Shabbat is called Shabbat Ha'Gadol. On the 14th, they slaughtered the lamb and marked their doorposts with its blood. That night God struck Egypt's firstborn, and Par'oh – himself a firstborn – sent the Jews out. After circling back following their three days, they drew Par'oh's pursuit, and both camps reached Yam Suf a week into freedom (the 21st), where the sea, split by a strong east wind, let the Jews cross and drowned the Egyptians.

This month's redemptive pattern recurs: 40 years later, Yehoshua began leading on the 7th of Nisan, 30 days after Moshe's death, and sent two spies to Yeriḥo that day; the nation crossed the Yarden three days later, on the 10th (2488/1273 BCE by tradition). Just as redemption came in Nisan, so will the final redemption. (Talmud Bavli, Rosh Hashana 11a)

The Month of Iyar

The month of Iyar is traditionally associated with blossoming. This has its basis in its Akkadian name, ajjāru, which refers to a "blossom" or "rosette", as well as its biblical name Ziv, meaning "radiance", reflecting the brilliance of spring flowers (I Melakhim 6:1).

This month may also be known for its sustenance and opportunity. In the first year after the Exodus, the Manna began to fall after the Jews complained about the lack of food on the 15th of Iyar (Shemot 16:1; 2448/1313 BCE by tradition). In the second year of wandering the desert, the 14th of this month was designated to give Jews another opportunity to bring the Korban Pesaḥ if they couldn't do so the month before (Bamidbar 9:11; 2449/1312 BCE by tradition), known today as Pesaḥ Sheni. Perhaps we have a hint of said sustenance this month as well: the new crop becomes permitted on the 16th of Nisan (after the 'Omer), making Iyar the first full month in which the new harvest may be eaten. The entire month falls within the 'Omer count, which continues each night.

Historically, this is also the month in which King Shelomo began construction of the First Temple (I Melakhim 6:1; 2928/833 BCE by tradition; c. 966 BCE by historians).

The Month of Sivan

Sivan means "season" or "appointed time" (from Akkadian simānu), fitting its place in the agricultural wheat harvest; it is named only once in Tanakh (Esther 8:9). This is reflected in Shavu'ot, associated with the Festival of the Harvest and first fruits of the wheat harvest. The events of Matan Torah fall within this period (2448/1313 BCE by tradition), though as the Abarbanel explains (Vayikra 23), the Torah established Shavu'ot for the wheat harvest rather than as an explicit commemoration of Matan Torah.

When calendar months were determined by witnesses, the flexibility of Nisan and Iyar permitted Shavu'ot (which was specifically the day after the full 7 weeks of the 'Omer) to fall on the 5th–7th of Sivan (Teshuvot Ha'Ribash 96:1). This is an interesting parallel to the historical date of Matan Torah itself, which could fall on two of those three days (Shabbat 86b): either the 6th of Sivan (the Sages) or the 7th (R' Yose). Under today's fixed calendar, Shavu'ot consistently falls on the 6th of Sivan.

The discrepancy between these two dates is explained (per the Ben Aryeh, Shabbat 87a) through the two-day perisha (separation) period commanded during the daytime of the 4th of Sivan. For Bnei Noaḥ (night follows day), the 4th and 5th formed the two days of perisha, placing Matan Torah on the 6th. However, for Bnei Yisrael (night precedes day), Moshe began the two-day count from Wednesday night, making the 5th and 6th the perisha days and placing Matan Torah on the 7th.

The Month of Tammuz

Long before adopting present names, the "fourth month" held a solemn weight tied to the sin of the Golden Calf. Centuries later, during the Babylonian exile, the name Tammuz was adopted – derived from Assyrian tammūzu (referencing the deity Dumuzi). Jewish awareness of this rite preceded the name, as Yeḥezkel's vision showed women at the Sanctuary's north gate "weeping for the Tammuz" (Yeḥezkel 8:14).

Though the deity and the fast share no intrinsic connection, the common thread is weeping – their weeping served as idolatrous worship (Radak, Rashi & Rambam), whereas ours expresses mourning for the Temple during this period called Bein Ha'Metzarim (בין המצרים). Spanning three weeks from the 17th of Tammuz until Tisha B'Av (תשעה באב), this period commemorates the breach of Jerusalem's walls (3829/69 CE by tradition; 70 CE by historians; the Yerushalmi includes the First Temple breach – cf. Yirmeyahu 39:2, the 9th). Four other tragedies occurred on the 17th:

  • Forty days post-Matan Torah (2448/1313 BCE by tradition), Moshe shattered the Tablets upon the Golden Calf's creation and dispatched the Levi'im against the idolaters (Midrashim add that the 'Erev Rav murdered Ḥur then).
  • Apostomos burned a Torah scroll.
  • An idol was set up in the Temple Sanctuary.
  • The daily sacrifices (Korban Ha'Tamid) ceased during the siege (from either priestly impurity or lack of sheep).

The theme of Tammuz is mishandled responsibility and its consequences – echoing the prophetic rebukes read during this season.

The Month of Av

מִשֶּׁנִּכְנַס אָב, מְמַעֲטִין בְּשִׂמְחָה
When [the month of] Av begins, one diminishes rejoicing.
Mishna Ta'anit 4:6

Av holds the year's saddest and happiest days, only six days apart (maybe even five). Its name, from the Akkadian abu, matches the Hebrew for "father"; we call it Menaḥem Av, looking toward consolation.

The sadness began in the desert: when the spies returned, the nation wept through the night of the 9th (2449/1312 BCE by tradition). Since that weeping was for nothing, God set the night aside for weeping for generations (Ta'anit 29a), as the Mishna records:

  • Both Temples were destroyed – the First by the Babylonians, burning into the 10th (3338/423 BCE by tradition; 586 BCE by historians), and the Second by the Romans (3829/69 CE by tradition; 70 CE by historians).
  • Betar fell, ending the Bar Kokhba revolt in a massacre of its residents (3881/121 CE by tradition; 135 CE by historians); a year later, Jerusalem was plowed over.

The list since is too long, and too painful, to print. Mourning builds from Rosh Ḥodesh until the fast (sunset to nightfall). Then, on the 15th, the mood turns: Tu B'Av ended the desert generation's dying, and the Mishna counts it among the year's happiest days, a day of matchmaking (Mishna Ta'anit 4:8; Ta'anit 30b).

The Month of Elul

אֲנִי לְדוֹדִי וְדוֹדִי לִי
I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine.
Shir Ha'Shirim 6:3

On Rosh Ḥodesh Elul, after 40 days of pleading for the nation to be forgiven, God commanded Moshe to ascend Har Sinai for the second tablets, which he brought down on Yom Kippur (Shemot 34; 2448/1313 BCE by tradition). This is the mindset of the month: recognizing the chances God gave us and preparing for mercy. Its name reflects this: the Akkadian ulūlu may relate to purification, while in Aramaic, אלל means "to search", as Onkelos renders the spies' scouting (ויאללון, Bamidbar 13:2) – a month for searching our deeds. Its letters are also the initials of the verse above.

Our custom is to recite Seliḥot starting after Rosh Ḥodesh (a minority also blow the shofar during the 13 attributes); Ashkenazim blow the shofar all month (except the eve of Rosh Hashana), reciting Seliḥot only at its end. The 25th is significant for two historical reasons:

  • Assuming the world was not created in Nisan, Rosh Hashana marks man's creation on the 6th day; counting back six days inclusively arrives at this day, when God started to give each item of the world its form.
  • Neḥemya finished rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem (Neḥemya 6:15; 3426/335 BCE by tradition; 445 BCE by historians).

Full Solar-Sphere Sunrise

The ideal form of the Halakhic day does not start until the uppermost edge of the solar sphere appears over the horizon. This moment is called הנץ החמה, and is sourced from the similar language in שיר השירים. Any significant time beforehand is considered less than ideal, although there is no requirement to be precise to the second – the rising of the sun is itself a range, so we are lenient within a window around it (on average, two minutes, both preceding and following).

This is best exemplified by prayer, where the transition between שמע and the עמידה is timed around the rising of the sun. It is this context, though, that demands the most precision for sunrise: do we time שמע itself to sunrise? Or is it the עמידה that's timed accordingly? Furthermore, this is assuming we're using the traditional definition for sunrise, as a contrast to the minority of later authorities who rule that the end of sunrise is to be used instead.

These disputes combine into several permutations – from the perspective of law, only one sunrise was meant, and it was for either the שמע or the עמידה. However, the gap between the two points provides enough time for the text of שמע and its following blessing to be said. Thus, one can fulfill two separate permutations – reciting the שמע when the sun's uppermost edge is above the horizon, and waiting until the full solar-sphere reveal to recite the עמידה. The other permutations are accommodated through the window range of sunrise.

This compromise is attributed to Ha'Rav Matzliaḥ Mazuz (סולת נקיה, עמוד רע"ג), but has received mixed receptions in the family of Ḥakham 'Ovadia. Although the idea was met warmly at first by both sons (including the 5764 printing of the Yalkut Yosef, siman 89 and all editions of the Halacha Berura, siman 58), it eventually became another point of contention between the brothers. The reason is that the Yalkut Yosef eventually returned to the standard practice of using the uppermost edge only, without being stringent for this second time. (The original לוח המאור from Yeshivat Ohr Hachaim presents only the uppermost-edge peak time, in accordance with the later Y"Y guidelines.)

To present these rulings here, we use several justifications: on top of representing the Halacha Berura's concluded ruling, even R' Yitzḥak Yosef would consider this time eligible for the "תפילה בהנץ" status since it's still close in time to the initial time for sunrise (and minute-level precision was never intended). However, since its use is disputed (and one would need to use the main time anyway for שמע), we've chosen to place these times separately from the main listing.

Pelag Ha'Minḥa – Ma'amar Mordekhi

Pelag Ha'Minḥa is defined as the midpoint of the time span from מנחה קטנה until the end of the day (ערב), occurring at 10¾ hours into the day. Since the day spans 12 hours, a shorter way of expressing this time is the inverse – 1¼ hours before the end of the day.

The dispute between the Halacha Berura and Yalkut Yosef is over the definition of ערב – is it sunset, or is it nightfall? The ramifications depend on how long Ben Ha'Shemashot lasts in that location.

On the surface, the Yalkut Yosef looks like a simple stringency – a way to be concerned for later opinions regarding blessings. However, almost every opinion the Yalkut Yosef relied on maintained compliance with the 12-hour structure the Talmud expected. In this structure, Ben Ha'Shemashot wasn't a gap following the day's end at sunset; rather, its conclusion was the day's endpoint. The other end of the day was extended as well, beginning at Dawn rather than sunrise.

If this detail was compromised, that means the seasonal minutes no longer correspond to the length of the day, of which a portion was assigned for Pelag purposes. These are the authorities who ruled accordingly (such as the Ramban, the Ra'ah, the Ritva & R' Ya'akov Emden), and there is a precedent for this legal re-evaluation (Peri Ḥadash, Kuntress Debei Shimsha, end of ספר מים חיים). Nevertheless, the vast majority agreed on the structure of the day being uncompromised.

The wording of the Yalkut Yosef indicates that the "later Pelag" was subject to this debate on the structure of the day. Following the precedent of those later authorities, one can even calculate it using the Geonic Nightfall rather than the proportional Nightfall of Rabbenu Tam (see the Ben Ish Ḥai, Year I, Vayakhel VIII; see also Ohr Letzion vol. 2 pg. 147 & the responsa of the Ma'amar Mordekhi vol. 4 siman 12).

The contrast to this structure, though, was the one implemented in the לוח המאור. It only considers the difference between sunset and nightfall as a gap, without the adjusted structure to come with it. Because of that, the difference between the two Pelag times depends on how long Ben Ha'Shemashot is for that day. For those looking to be ideal in their conduct regarding Pelag (such as avoiding תרתי דסתרי), this makes the stringency overbearing depending on the time of year.

Thus, although we've put the official לוח המאור time as the primary representative of the Y"Y opinion, we've included this alternative calculation in these back pages. To avoid confusion with any other delay to nightfall, we've named this after the Ma'amar Mordekhi, which spelled out this opinion with all its implications (such as midday not being equal to the solar midday).

Full-Length Rabbenu Tam List

The transition from day to night is the same for Rabbenu Tam as it is for everyone; after "sunset", wait ¾ of a mil and Nightfall occurs. However, sunset itself is redefined – occurring 3¼ mil after our sunset.

Considering each mil is 18 minutes, this makes Rabbenu Tam's Nightfall as late as seventy-two minutes after our sunset at baseline level. To stay consistent with how Talmudic time is defined, this length changes based on the location's latitude and the day's length.

Our interpretation of these later Nightfall opinions is only for their stringent aspects (such as waiting to perform the new commandments of the following day, or performing acts that would have been forbidden on the previous day); leniencies (relative to our standard Halakha) coming with this calculation are forbidden to be practiced.
(There is also no need to be stringent on Rabbinic times.)

However, compliance with such a standard is taxing – one day's sunset to the next day's nightfall is no longer 24 hours with the length of Ben Ha'Shemashot, but rather an additional 58.5 minutes (with the same adjustments indicated above). If we're to be consistent with our standards of adjustments, this length could very well exceed what most would consider "reasonable" for a stringency.

Thus, there are compromise calculations suggested, that still accommodate the Talmudic numbers and the alternative application. The first method is to rule leniently during Rabbenu Tam's Ben Ha'Shemashot, using the principle of "majority opinions" – from 58.5 [adjusted-seasonal] minutes onwards, everyone (with the 18-minute mil) holds it is either certain night or doubtful night, which combine for leniency. The alternative (implemented in our calendar's weekly pages) is to limit the 72 minutes to clock minutes, and only use the seasonal result when they're shorter. (Other forms of providing lenient times for Rabbenu Tam are contrary to our ideology of Zemanim being a baseline from Israel and increasing when moving north.)

The Month of Tishri

בֶּחֳדָשִׁים הַשְּׁבִיעִי חָבִיב, "בַחֹדֶשׁ הַשְּׁבִיעִי בְּאֶחָד לַחֹדֶשׁ"
Of the months, the seventh is the beloved; "In the seventh month, on the first day"
Yalkut Shimoni, Parashat Yitro 276

Tishri is the month that starts various Jewish cycles, including the Sabbatical year, vows, planting (for Orlah) and vegetation (tithes and terumot). According to R' Eliezer, the world (determined by mankind) was created in this month – or at the very least, creation as a concept occurred for God during this month (see Rabbenu Tam on Rosh Hashanah 27a; combine with the resolution of Resh Lakish on Ḥagigah 12a, Rashi on Bereshit 1:1, and Bereshit Rabbah 12:4 for a complete explanation). The name itself reinforces this status as a starting point – it's derived from the Akkadian word tašrītu, which literally means 'beginning' from the root šurrū (to begin, open, or initiate).

The themes of the month's holidays are judgment, repentance and the acknowledgement that God is our king. The holidays that start the month are Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, with the Ten Days of Repentance connecting them – focusing on our judgment. Then we move to Sukkot and the judgment of the rain, a theme reflected as early as the month's initial name (איתנים - see I Kings 8:2). This all culminates in Shemini 'Atzeret, which we've also made the day for completing the Torah cycle.

The Month of Marḥeshvan

In the Tanakh (I Kings 6:38), this month is referred to as Yeraḥ Bul, a name rooted in seasonal shifts such as the withering of grass (baleh), the mixing of fodder (bolelin), and the harvesting of produce (yevul). The name is also associated with the onset of rain—forming clods of earth (bolot) and recalling the Great Flood (mabul)—which mirrors the current practice in Israel of requesting rain beginning on the 7th of the month (ברך עלינו), with historical fasts held if none fell by the 17th.

The name Marḥeshvan (often shortened to Ḥeshvan) instead denotes the month's numerical order, deriving from the Akkadian warḫu samnu ("eighth month"). Linguistically, warḫu corresponds to the Hebrew yerach (month) and samnu relates to shemonah (eight). While the prefix Mar is often interpreted as "bitter" due to the month's lack of festivals, it also means "drop," (such as its use in Isaiah 40:15) signifying the welcome arrival of the autumn rains. As a complete contrast, the Aramaic definition behind the prefix "Mar" is "master", representing the construction of the First Temple (despite the bitter delay of its dedication) and the eventual repayment through future celebrations.

The Month of Kislev

The strength of Kislev is shown as early as Ḥaggai, who encouraged the nation to rebuild the Temple, promising prosperity and military victory over its era's enemies. (Ḥaggai 2:10; this occurred on the 24th of Kislev, 3242/520 BCE) Further strength was shown by the later prophet, 'Ezra, who gathered the nation for three days and strengthened Torah observance while simultaneously dissolving interfaith marriages. ('Ezra 10:9; this occurred on the 20th of Kislev, 3414/348 BCE)

The area of strength most famous from this month was the war against the Greek empire, later celebrated in the form of Ḥanukkah. Although the entire story spanned several years, the milestone events were performed in this month.

  • On the 15th of Kislev 3595/167 BCE, the "abomination of desolation" was set on the altar and multiple idols were set in the streets of Judea.
  • On the 25th of Kislev 3595/167 BCE, idolatrous sacrifices were offered on the altar.
  • Three years later, on the 25th of Kislev 3598/164 BCE, the Kohanim were able to resume their service. The only sealed oil jug found was used to light the candles for the following eight days.

The name Kislev itself may echo this theme of strength. Some derive it from the Akkadian root kislu (kaslu), meaning "inner strength."

The Month of Tevet

Following Ḥanukkah, Tevet serves as the representative of the winter season, whether as its climax or beginning (See Bava Metzia 106b on the six-season cycle division). The winter solstice found in this month (as titled in the Hebrew – Tekufat Tevet) demonstrates the qualities of the winter seasons (specifically across the northern hemisphere): shortest days from sunrise to sunset, due to the sun's low path across the sky. This detail could be reflected in the original name of the month, which is derived from Akkadian ṭebētu ("sunk").

Spiritually, this solar gloom reflects a deeper Kabbalistic theme. According to the Zohar (Yitro 13:241) and Ma'aseh Rokeach (commentary to Mishnah Ta'anit 4:6), Tevet is one of three months governed by stern judgment (Dina Kasya). In the division of the months between the patriarchs, Tevet fell into the portion of Esau, temporarily giving power to the Sitra Achara (the side of impurity).

Within the month itself, this spiritual vulnerability manifests on the 8th-10th days, known for the tragedies that befell – namely the translation of the Torah into Greek, the passing of 'Ezra (and with it, the leadership he represented), and the siege of Jerusalem marked by the fast of צום העשירי (10th of Tevet). It was on this month, though, that decades later would King Ahashverosh take Esther as his wife (Megillat Esther, 2:16), representing the Purim story and the redemption that followed suit.

The Month of Shevat

שנשמע בסורות טובות

The agriculture-influence of Shevat is found as early as its name, with its Akkadian source (šabāṭu) referring to the harsh rains that come with the solar season of this month. We also know (from R' Hai Gaon, Teshuvot HaGeonim [Harkavy], §243) that the solar season coincides with the Arabic al-jamra al-thāniya (the second ember/coal) period, where the trees become moist and begin to absorb water, thus reviving them. Hence, it is fitting for the New Year of the Trees to fall in this season, celebrated on the 15th day known as ט"ו בשבט. The exact date follows the ruling of בית הלל; see Mishna Rosh Hashana 1:1 for the full discussion, including the opinion of בית שמאי

This month started the final speech of Moses, addressing the nation before Joshua replaced his leadership. These teachings would ultimately be written in the form of the Book of Deuteronomy, often called Mishneh Torah.

The Month of Adar I

The months of the year are determined by the lunar cycle, which falls short by 11 days from the solar calendar. This desync causes a problem for Jewish holidays, which have seasonal placements despite the date being ruled from the lunar month. Thus, we add the month of Adar I to the lunar calendar. With Hillel's calendar cycle originating in 358-9 CE, the institution of this month was no longer dependent on agricultural concerns and astronomical calculations (which required a Sanhedrin), instead just a division of seven leap years and twelve "ordinary" years, collectively becoming a nineteen-year cycle.

We've kept the month of Adar II for the respective festival of Purim (to keep the redemption of Purim close to the redemption from Egypt) and the recital of the Arba Parashiot (which are dependent on Purim). Nevertheless, the 14th and 15th of Adar I were titled [Shushan-]Purim Katan, observed by refraining from Taḥanun, eulogies, or fasts.

The ramifications of this month affect Hiloulot, vows, and birthdays (affecting Jewish adulthood - the bar mitzvah status). (See the discussion in Talmud Yerushalmi, Megillah 7a-7b)

The Month of Adar (II)

מִשֶּׁנִּכְנַס אֲדָר מַרְבִּין בְּשִׂמְחָה
When [the month of] Adar begins, one increases rejoicing.
Talmud Bavli, Ta'anit 29a

Adar centers on Purim, marking the overturning of Haman's decree to annihilate the Jews of Persia. Although Haman himself had already been executed, his decree remained in force, so the Jews were authorized to defend themselves. They fought on the 13th of Adar throughout the empire and rested on the 14th; in Susa they fought through the 14th and rested on the 15th.

These events are recorded in the Book of Esther. Purim falls on the 15th in walled cities like Susa, and the 14th elsewhere. Observance includes reading the Scroll of Esther, giving to the poor, sending food gifts, and a festive meal (with wine - drink a bit more than usual, then sleep; see B"Y 695:2). The Fast of Esther, held beforehand, commemorates the fasting and prayer that preceded the fighting.

More broadly, Adar marks victories over persecutions from both Temple eras: Nebuchadnezzar, destroyer of the First Temple, died on the 25th (Rashi, II Kings 25:27); and the Second Temple-era Battle of Adasa is commemorated as Nicanor Day on the 13th (1 Maccabees 7:26–50).

The Month of Nisan

הַחֹ֧דֶשׁ הַזֶּ֛ה לָכֶ֖ם רֹ֣אשׁ חֳדָשִׁ֑ים רִאשׁ֥וֹן הוּא֙ לָכֶ֔ם לְחָדְשֵׁ֖י הַשָּׁנָֽה:
This month shall be to you the head of the months; to you it shall be the first of the months of the year.
Exodus 12:2

Nisan is a month of new beginnings. Its name derives from the Akkadian root Nisānu, and it marks several fresh starts:

  • It opens spring (hence its biblical name, חודש האביב), ending winter and allowing new produce to grow.
  • Royal reigns are dated from this month when timestamping royal decrees. (Mishna Rosh Hashana 1:1)

Most significant is the Jewish nation's own transition from slavery in Egypt to freedom, commemorated as Passover. The nation commemorated the new moon of Wednesday night as the start of Nisan (and thus, the start of the Jewish month inauguration process). The following Shabbat (the 10th), the nation was commanded to tie a lamb to their bedpost, alarming the Egyptians into internal quarrel; this Shabbat is called Shabbat HaGadol. On the 14th, they slaughtered the lamb and marked their doorposts with its blood. That night God struck Egypt's firstborns, and Pharaoh – himself a firstborn – sent the Jews out. After circling back following their three days, they drew Pharaoh's pursuit, and both camps reached the Red Sea a week into freedom (the 21st), where the split sea from the great winds let the Jews cross and drowned the Egyptians.

This month's redemptive pattern recurs: 40 years later, Yehoshua began leading on the 7th of Nisan, 30 days after Moshe's death, and sent spies to Yericho three days after that. Just as these conquests happened in Nisan, so will the final redemption. (Talmud Bavli, Rosh Hashanah 11b)

The Month of Iyar

The month of Iyar follows the previous month of Nisan, and is traditionally associated with blossoming. This has its basis in its Akkadian name, ajjāru, which refers to a 'blossom' or 'rosette', as well as its biblical name Ziv, meaning 'radiance', reflecting the brilliance of spring flowers.

This month may be known also for its sustenance and opportunity – in the second year of wandering the desert, the 14th of this month was designated to give Jews another opportunity to offer the Passover offering if they couldn't do so the month before. The sustenance provided came in the form of the Manna, which fell after the Jews complained about the lack of food on the 15th of Iyar. Perhaps we have somewhat of a hint of said sustenance this month, as the previous year's grain becomes Yashan on the 16th of Nisan, leaving a surplus of grain for months to benefit from (with Iyar being the first full month of the Yashan grain).

Historically, this is also the month in which King Solomon began construction of the First Temple.

The Month of Sivan

Sivan means “season” or “appointed time” (from Akkadian simānu), fitting its place in the agricultural wheat harvest. This is reflected in Shavu'ot, associated with the Festival of the Harvest and first fruits of the wheat harvest. The events of Matan Torah fall within this period, though as the Abarbanel explains (Leviticus 23), the Torah established Shavu'ot for the wheat harvest rather than as an explicit commemoration of Matan Torah.

When calendar months were determined by witnesses, the flexibility of Nisan and Iyar permitted Shavu'ot (which was specifically the day after the full 7 weeks of the 'Omer) to fall on the 5th–7th of Sivan (Teshuvot HaRibash 96:1). This is an interesting parallel to the historical date of Matan Torah itself, which could fall on two of those three days (Shabbat 86b): either the 6th of Sivan (the Sages) or the 7th (R' Yose). Under today's fixed calendar, Shavu'ot consistently falls on the 6th of Sivan.

The discrepancy between these two dates is explained (as per the Ben Aryeh, Shabbat 87a) through the two-day perisha (separation) period commanded during the daytime of the 4th of Sivan. For Bnei Noah (night follows day), the 4th and 5th formed the two days of perisha, placing Matan Torah on the 6th. However, for Bnei Yisrael (night precedes day), Moshe began the two-day count from Wednesday night, making the 5th and 6th the perisha days and placing Matan Torah on the 7th.

The Month of Tammuz

Long before adopting present names, the "fourth month" held a solemn weight tied to the sin of the Golden Calf. Centuries later, during the Babylonian exile, the name Tammuz was adopted—derived from Assyrian tammūzu (referencing the deity Dumuzi). Jewish awareness of this rite preceded the name, as Yeḥezkel's vision showed women at the Sanctuary's north gate "weeping for the Tammuz" (Yeḥezkel 8:14).

Though the deity and the fast share no intrinsic connection, the common thread is weeping—their weeping served as idolatrous worship (Radak, Rashi & Rambam), whereas ours expresses mourning for the Temple during this period called Bein HaMetzarim (בין המצרים). Spanning three weeks from the 17th of Tammuz until Tisha B'Av (תשעה באב), this period commemorates the breach of Jerusalem's walls in 69 CE (and the First Temple breach per the Talmud Yerushalmi; see Jeremiah 39:2 for the opinion of the 9th of Tammuz instead). Four other tragedies occurred on the 17th:

  • Forty days post-Matan Torah, Moshe shattered the Tablets upon the Golden Calf's creation and dispatched the Levi'im against the idolaters. (Midrashic sources add that Ḥur was murdered by the 'Erev Rav in this event).
  • Apostomos burned a Torah scroll.
  • An idol was set up in the Temple Sanctuary.
  • The daily sacrifices (Korban HaTamid) ceased during the siege (from either priestly impurity or lack of sheep).

The theme of Tammuz is mishandled responsibility and its consequences—echoing the prophetic rebukes read during this season.

The Month of Av

מִשֶּׁנִּכְנַס אָב, מְמַעֲטִין בְּשִׂמְחָה
When [the month of] Av begins, one diminishes rejoicing.
Mishnah Ta'anit 4:6

The month of Av transitions from its 9th day to its 15th - the contrast between the saddest and happiest, separated only by 6 days (maybe even just 5).

The Midrashim explain that due to the Jews "crying for no reason" on the first Ninth of Av, God will provide reasons for mourning for years to come. Indeed, the list below includes only events listed in the Mishnah, but the full list that extends to our day would be too long (and too painful):

  • The Jewish nation wept over entering the Land of Israel, when the spies came back with the negative testimony. God decreed that the generation would die in the desert throughout its 40-year wandering.
  • Both Temples were destroyed, by the Babylonians and Romans respectively. For the First Temple specifically, the burning extended into the 10th.
  • The Bar Kokhba revolt against the Romans (132–135 CE) ended in defeat: Betar, an important Jewish stronghold, fell to the Romans in 135 CE, leaving its Jewish residents butchered. One year afterwards, the Romans plowed over Jerusalem, destroying hopes of its rebuilding [for that generation].

The Month of Elul

After 40 days of pleading for the Jewish nation to be forgiven, God commands Moshe to return to Har Sinai to receive the second tablets. This is the mindset of the month of Elul - the season to be aware of all the chances God gave us throughout the year and prepare for mercy.

These practices, by definition, differ between the Sephardic and Ashkenazi communities. Our custom is to recite the prayers of repentence (Seliḥot), starting after Rosh Ḥodesh. The practice by Ashkenazim utilizes the shofar (usually reserved for Rosh Hashanah) throughout the entire month (except the eve of Rosh Hashanah). There is overlap between the two; a minority of Sephardic congregations blow the shofar during the recital of the 13 attributes, whereas Ashkenazim also recite Seliḥot at the end of the month.

The 25th of the month is significant for two historical reasons:

  • Assuming the world was not created in Nisan, Rosh Hashanah is called as such because it was the day man was created. Since that occurred on the 6th day of creation, counting back six days inclusively from Rosh Hashanah arrives at this day, when God started to give each item of the world its form.
  • It's the day Nehemiah finished rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem after its original destruction.

Full Solar-Sphere Sunrise

The ideal form of the Halachic day does not start until the uppermost edge of the solar sphere appears over the horizon. This moment is called הנץ החמה, and is sourced from the similar language in שיר השרים. Anytime beforehand is considered unideal.

This is best exemplified by prayer, where transition between שמע to the שמונה עשרה are timed around the rising of the sun. It is this context, though, that creates the most precision for sunrise: do we time שמע itself to sunrise? Or is it the שמונה עשרה that's timed accordingly? Furthermore, this is assuming we're using the traditional definition for sunrise, as a minority of later authorities rule that the end of sunrise is to be used instead.

These disputes exist in permutation - from the perspective of law, only one sunrise was meant and it was for either the שמע or the עמידה. However, there is enough of a gap between the two points of sunrise that one could say the שמע at the earlier one and finish the blessings by the full solar reveal, thus starting the עמידה at the "second sunrise". This definitionally fulfills two different permutations, and the other forms of the permutation are also accounted for since they fit in the range of sunrise (rather than the exact points, which is still permissible according to the law).

This is the compromise of HaRav Matzliach Mazuz, introduced to the dynasty of Ḥakham Ovadia by the Halacha Berura. The scheduling they give is based on a combination of various texts, such as the Rambam's "6 minutes until the full solar sphere sunrise" with ammendments by the Vilna Gaon. Of course, the numbers used there were approximate in nature - however, using our current scientific knowledge on "refraction" and other details that affect the appearance of the sun on the horizon, we are able to give unprecedented precision.

Our generation of the time is layered on top of ChaiTables's Visual Sunrise time (provided Visual Sunrise is available for your location), based on the instructions of HaRav Mazuz (עיין ב"סולת נקיה" עמוד רע"ג). At the time of writing, the service does not offer a way of determining the end of sunrise (we hope to collaborate and change this in the near future). What we've done is take the time difference between sea-level sunrise and sea-level full-solar-reveal sunrise and apply it on top of the Visual Sunrise time.

The importance of this time is only as long as one believes these times are accurate to the second, something that is impossible for any printed calendar to be. In reality, there is a range of sunrise, and one can pray even beforehand. Furthermore, the tradition has already been set to side-step this stringency, and rely fully on the primary sunrise (even if it may be earlier than the sea-level one)

Pelag Ha'Minḥa - Ma'amar Mordekhi

Pelag Ha'Minḥa is defined as the latter half of the time span between מנחה קטנה until the end of the day (ערב), occurring at 10¾ hours into the day. Considering how the day spans 12 hours, a shorter form of expressing this time is in the inverse - that being 1¼ hours from the end of the day.

The dispute between the Halacha Berura and Yalkut Yosef is between the definition of ערב - is it sunset, or is it nightfall? The ramifications depend on however long Ben Hashemashot could get in that location.

On the surface, the Yalkut Yosef looks like a simple stringency - a way to be concerned for later opinions regarding blessings. However, almost every opinion the Yalkut Yosef relied on maintained a compliance with the 12-hour structure the Gemara expected. This structure wasn't serving Ben Hashemashot as a gap from sunset, but rather, an endpoint to the day. The other side of the endpoint was extended as well, being at Dawn rather than sunrise.

If this detail was compromised, that means the seasonal minutes no longer correspond to the length of the day, of which a portion was assigned for Pelag purposes. These are authorities that ruled as such practice (such as the Ramban, Ra'ah, Ritva & Rav Yaakov Emden), and there is a precedent for this legal re-evaluation (Peri Ḥadash, Kuntress Debei Shimsha, end of ספר מים חיים). Nevertheless, the vast majority agreed on the structure of the day being uncompromised.

The wording of the Yalkut Yosef indicates that the "later Pelag" was subject to this debate on the structure of the day. Just like the later precedent in authorities, one can even do it using the Geonic Nightfall rather than the proportional Nightfall of Rabbenu Tam (see the Ben Ish Ḥai, Year I, Vayakhel VIII; see also Ohr Letzion vol. 2 pg. 147 & the responsas of the Ma'amar Mordechai vol. 4 siman 12).

The contrast to this structure, though, was the one implemented in the לוח אור החיים. They only consider the difference between sunset and nightfall as a gap, without the adjusted structure to come with it. Because of that, the difference between the two Pelag's are dependent on however long Ben Hashemashot is for that day. For those looking to act ideal regarding Pelag (such as תרתי דסתרי), this makes the stringency overbearing depending on the time of year.

Thus, although we've put the official לוח אור החיים time as the primary representative of the Y"Y opinion, we've included this alternative calculation in these back pages. To avoid any confusion for any type of delay to nightfall, we've named this on the Ma'amar Mordekhi, who spelt out this opinion with all its implications (such as midday not being equal to the solar-midday).

Full-Length Rabbenu Tam List

The transition from day to night is the same for Rabbenu Tam as it is for everyone; after "sunset", wait ¾th's of a mil and Nightfall occurs. However, sunset itself is redefined - occuring 3¼ mil after our sunset.

Considering each mil is 18 minutes, this makes Rabbenu Tam's Nightfall as long as seventy-two minutes after our sunset at baseline level. Through being consistent with defining Talmudic time, this length changes based on the location's latitude and the day's length.

Our interpretation of these later Nightfall interpretations is only for their stringent aspects (such as waiting to perform the new commandments of the following day, or performing acts that would have been forbidden on the previous day); leniencies (relative to our standard Halakha) coming with this calculation are forbidden to be practiced.
(There is also no need to be stringent on Rabbinic times)

However, compliance with such a standard is taxing - one day's sunset to the next day's nightfall is no longer 24 hours with the length of Ben Hashemashot, but rather an additional 58.5 minutes (with the same adjustments indicated above). If we're to be consistent with our standards of adjustments, this length could very well exceed what most would consider "reasonable" for a stringency.

Thus, there are compromise calculations suggested, that still accommodate the Talmudic numbers and the alternative application. The first method is to rule leniently during Rabbenu Tam's Ben Hashemashot, using the principal of "majority opinions" - from 58.5 [adjusted-seasonal] minutes onwards, everyone (with the 18-minute mil) holds it is either certain night or doubtful night, which combine for leniency. The alternative (implemented in our calendar's weekly pages) is to limit the 72 minutes to clock minutes, and only use the seasonal result when they're shorter. (Other forms of providing lenient times for Rabbenu Tam are contrary to our ideology of Zemanim being a baseline from Israel and increasing when moving north)
